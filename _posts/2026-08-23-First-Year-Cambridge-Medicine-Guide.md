Quite a few people have asked me have asked me for advice for first year medicine, so I thought I'd write it up. A lot of what I have to say applies specifically to Cambridge Medicine, but I believe that the general sentiment still applies to Medicine at other universities too (in terms of work-life balance).

Why should you listen to my advice? Well... It's purely your choice at the end of the day. If you are determined to try and top the year, then this article is not for you, enjoy your delusion, but if you're looking to enjoy your first year and still improve as a person whilst studying something intense like medicine, then I think that what I have to say will be of greater value. 

### On Tripos:
Did I top the year? No. I got a decent 2.1. Could I have done better? Yes, I did a bit worse than I expected, but I am by no means dissatisfied by my score.

Why? Because... it literally doesn't matter.

To graduate as a doctor in any medschool in the UK, your medicine degree is pass or fail. Cambridge is no exception, at the end of your six years you get a medicine degree - just a medicine degree. The special Cambridge element is that you also get a second degree from Cambridge at the end of your third year, which has a grade (1st, 2.1, 2,2, 3rd etc.), and so for the first 3 years of your medicine degree you essentially sit 2 types of exams: the 2nd MB ones (pass or fail exams, which you have to pass to become a doctor), and the tripos ones (essays which you could fail with no repercussions). Your tripos degree is weighted 50% to your essays and 50% to your 2nd MB score.

There are 2 reasons to care about what you get in your tripos degree:
1. If you don't want to be a doctor you can leave Cambridge Medicine at the end of your third year to go into research or finance or something else with a Cambridge undergraduate degree, and so just like most degrees a higher grade increases your employability.
2. To some degree you have to do decently to get the intercalation that you want in your first year.

But none of these are valid reasons for caring a lot about Tripos if you want to be a doctor.
1. With regards to the first reason, if you want to be a doctor, your tripos degree counts for nothing. There is nowhere on your specialty application form that asks for what you got on your intercalated degree, it counts for **0** points.
2. With regards to the second reason, you need a high-ish 2.1 to get into the most competitive intercalation (which is management), and for almost everything else a 2.1 will comfortably get you what you want. (Cambridge medicine students seem to have extremely poor taste in intercalation options too, you're telling me you chose management or another year of arbitrary science when you could do... almost any subject). Also it's worth saying that your intercalation option doesn't affect your chances of getting into a specialty either, it's also completely arbitrary.
3. Some people say that your first year grade and second year grade can help you get the intercalation you want, others say it's just your second year grade, others say it depends, but one thing that is for certain is that your first year grade counts to **0%** of your tripos degree score... None... Nada... Only your second and third years count.

Why am I saying all this? Because when you study medicine at Cambridge everyone will try and gaslight you into thinking your tripos score is some judgement day level event, but it isn't. It's pretty pointless.

The best piece of advice I can give you is to not let yourself be gaslit into caring about tripos. Don't worry about it at all. Worry about putting in a decent amount of effort - and by that I mean doing your due diligence. Study to be a good doctor, don't study for a grade, and the grade will take care of itself. 

Some people in older years (particularly second years and a very small number of obstinate older years) will tell you that you should lock in for tripos and that it's so important, because it helps you do x,y,z but these are pretty much all made up reasons, used for their own self-justification often. And supervisors have obvious incentives for you to do well in tripos (it makes them look good), but again it doesn't matter. 

You'll be able to find research easily at Cambridge, it's not being gate-kept by your tripos grade, and anyone who actually cares about you, all your decent friends, will judge you by the content of your character, not an arbitrary grade.

Does this all mean that you shouldn't study? No. Work consistently and diligently, but don't make medicine all consuming, and never let your work compromise your values.

The heedless, blind following the blind, no purpose in life, sheep mentality of Cambridge medicine students is very draining to deal with, but know what you want out of life and what's important to you, and don't let their docile herd mentality pervade your own thought.

Ok that's enough about Tripos.

### Actual Cambridge Medicine Advice - general:
- Don't preread anything except for the FAB handbook, before going into the dissection room (not FAB lectures those are different, don't preread for those).
- Don't bother taking notes in lectures, everything you need to know is on the slides or handout, just focus and pay attention, they say wayyy too much for you to keep up and keep paying attention simultaneously.
- Don't bother making an anki deck, just use the ones the older years' have made if you have to use them, and don't do Anki religiously, the small details it encourages you to obsess over are very low yield compared to the understanding (which Anki decks can't teach you well).

### Actual Cambridge Medicine Advice - subjects:
You have 3 subjects and 3 chores. FAB, MIMS and HOM are your three subjects and SECHI, FEBP and PFP are your chores. Don't go to any SECHI lectures unless they interest you (I say that as someone who loves the humanities), and don't go to any FEBP lectures. Do the bare minimum to pass SECHI, FEBP and PFP and you'll  be fine.

(FAB = anatomy, HOM = homeostasis/physiology, MIMS = biochemistry, SECHI = ethics, FEBP = stats, PFP = GP Placements)

Well... I say don't go to those lectures, but I should really say don't feel like you have to go. It's not a productive use of your time, but realistically the hour you're getting from not going is also not a productive use of your time. When people say don't go, they don't mean that you're hurting yourself by going, rather that it's completely arbitrary as to whether you go or not. 

### Actual Cambridge Medicine Advice - FAB:
Ok more subject specific...

FAB consists of 3 parts. In terms of workload FAB is organised as follows:
- 80% of FAB is the weekly grind/cycle of learning what's in the FAB handbook and going to DR sessions
- 10% of FAB is being absolutely locked in during your incredibly high yield applied anatomy sessions
- 3% of FAB is the pretty laid back miscellaneous FAB lectures (and by that I mean just relaxing and enjoying them and doing some light lecture handout reading when revising)
- 7% of FAB is your embryology lectures/content.

### Actual Cambridge Medicine Advice - FAB - the handbook/DR:
FAB is a subject where you can spend a lot of time learning absolutely nothing. The FAB handbook is incredibly dense and unhelpful as a teaching method. Imperial medics get access to an app called "Complete Anatomy" that I think is an incredibly good investment. I used to open the FAB handbook, and go through it with complete anatomy open, adding the anatomical features and seeing their relations. 

*Note: One of my friends has pointed out to me that Visible Body Suite, which apparently us Cambridge medics get for free, is amazing too (instead of Complete Anatomy). I've personally never used it, but I would check it out before cashing out on a Complete Anatomy subscription. Directions to accessing it for free are linked [here](https://bio.lib.cam.ac.uk/pdn-library/electronic-resources).*

- Sam Webster makes good introductory videos that are quite easy to watch whilst you're eating a meal: [Sam Webster's Youtube Channel](https://www.youtube.com/@SamWebster)

- This guy has really good anatomy videos for the pelvic diaphram and the long list of arteries in the abdomen: [About Medicine](https://www.youtube.com/@aboutmedicine)

- Kenhub is not bad too: [Kenhub](https://www.youtube.com/@Kenhub)

- I hear good things about teach me anatomy [Teach Me Anatomy](https://teachmeanatomy.info)

- Here's a list of all the muscles in the handbook: [FAB muscle Spreadsheet](/downloads/FAB-Muscles.xlsx)

- And I also hear good things about the dissection videos on moodle

- Don't use anatomedia, it's rubbish

- And don't forget about using AI. Sometimes you get super compact sentences in the handbook that are just a relations of words that you've never heard of, which is effectively gibberish, and putting it into AI like chatGPT or Claude and just breaking down what every word means is very helpful.

So essentially in my opinion the methodology for FAB should be this. Every week you have 2 sessions in the dissection room where you go through a part of the body: from upper limb to axilla to neck to thorax to abdomen to lower limb. Every week look at the entirety of what it going to be dissected that week, not just by your group, but by all the groups. Look at all of it in the dissection manual for that week.  
Then go through the sections of the handbook that talk about that stuff systematically. Take it sentence by sentence, word by word, it takes ages. What I used to do was read a word then go to complete anatomy and search it up and look at that anatomical feature, then take the next word you don't understand and do the same and see the relations between multiple elements.  
Don't think of the handbook as being written in plain English, think of it as how one would read logical notation, or set theory: 
![alt text](/images/2026-08-23-First-Year-Cambridge-Medicine-Guide/image-1-1.webp)
I'm really grateful for the opportunity I had at school to study the philosophy of language, because it let me quickly realise that most of the handbook isn't designed to convey ideas, it's merely designed to show relations between different objects. This is where putting sentences into chatGPT might help, because sometimes seeing the same information from a different angle helps you visualise it better.  
Also with NO exceptions, do not click on any anatomedia links in the handbook. It is a rubbish website that contains loads of facts that are either completely useless, unrelated or arbitrary. The only examinable parts of the handbook are the things in the handbook directly, not on links.  
Sometimes I'd get stuck of overwhelmed and sometimes I would just read sentences over and over again and it would mean nothing, at this point I'd advise taking a break and going for a walk, or eating a meal or doing something else. Sometimes this happened to me at the beginning of a work session and in that case I'd just open youtube and watch a few videos on the topic to get a general idea of what's going on. There are quite a few conceptual barriers like the difference between the greater and lesser omentum, that make the surrounding topics really difficult to understand without understanding what the heck is going on conceptually, and sometimes youtube videos help here. I think that having a supervisor or someone in an older year explain these concepts simply and visually helps too, so I'd recommend asking a supervisor or someone older too. (Often people in your also have no clue what's going on, even if they sound like they do - this is usually because they merely rote memorised anki cards).

Ok so you've gone through the handbook with a 3D app and youtube videos and some AI and it's taken you a really long time (unfortunately that's how it is), then you go to the dissection room and use that to consolidate your learning not just to learn stuff the first time. Ask questions, super obvious and easy ones to the demonstrators, it's ok they literally can't do anything to discipline or reprimand you, and my year has set the bar for questions really low anyways (you're welcome). Some demonstrators give mid/rubbish answers, others will give amazing answers, *and others will spend 1 hour talking about latin, textual criticism in the new testament with you, then tell the next group that you accomplished nothing **even though** he was also clearly thoroughly enjoying your conversation 💔*...

 Sometimes you won't understand something by studying it yourself, and then demonstrators will make it seem super easy, and as a result you'll be tempted to just go in and learn from the DR first in the future. Don't do that. A much more healthy approach is to not obsess over details in your prereading and leave stuff you're struggling with for the DR if you're stuck, but keep pre-reading the FAB-handbook!!! Otherwise you'll fall super far behind quickly!!!

That's everything that comes to mind right now for the handbook/DR stuff.

### Actual Cambridge Medicine Advice - FAB - Embryology:
Embryology is super easy, the trouble is... the lecturers are... bad... especially the earlier ones. 

At St John's we had some amazing embryology supervisors who made us understand just how easy it really all is when you cut the nonsense, and so I'd recommend taking a step back and not trying to learn it from rewatching the lectures.

Ninja nerd has some brilliant embryology videos: [Ninja Nerd ](https://www.youtube.com/channel/UC6QYFutt9cluQ3uSM963_KQ), but sometimes what he says might disagree with the notes, so always take what the notes say to be true.


90% of Embryology is simple spacial understanding, so try to understand everything diagrammatically like this amazing heart development video: [heart development animation ](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RpZHiwkFUM4). Oftentimes drawing diagrams help too:
![alt text](/images/2026-08-23-First-Year-Cambridge-Medicine-Guide/image-2.webp)

The problem with embryology (and lots of FAB), is that often something only makes sense from one specific 2D view/plane/angle, and the plane which you have to focus on changes throughout development. If you can master knowing which angle to approach embryological changes from, spatially, you'll find it really easy.

The other 10% is just memorising names and relations, which can actually be really simple, but the lecturers made them sound super complicated (at least to us):
![alt text](/images/2026-08-23-First-Year-Cambridge-Medicine-Guide/2026-08-23_14-43-43-1.webp)
It's worth mentioning though that embryology isn't that high yield for FAB as a whole. I think it's interesting, and it is certainly useful for essays and some MCQs, but don't prioritise it over learning core FAB content (i.e. the handbook).

### Actual Cambridge Medicine Advice - FAB - FAB Lectures:
I sat back and enjoyed these during term time, and to revise I just watched them on 2 times speed, don't stress, they're not free marks or anything, but they're not the be all and end all.

### Actual Cambridge Medicine Advice - FAB - Applied Anatomy Lectures:
These on the other hand... Are super high yield. Probably the most important thing you learn in first year. Go to all of these, and lock in. Don't take notes, the notes they give you after the lectures are amazing 10/10, just pay super close attention to them. The lecturer is amazing, his lectures are amazing, genuinely the best part of first year.

Everyone uses AI for their own cases unfortunately, but don't stress about prepping too much, they're just a bit of fun, the guy who runs these lectures is super chill too.

The lectures are NOT recorded, which is more cause to pay attention, (that being said the notes are 10/10). If only all of Cambridge med was like this...

### FAB...

That's all of FAB I think? It is definitely the most time consuming subject by a long mile, the handbook is a real grind, but it's probably the most relevant to being a doctor.

### Actual Cambridge Medicine Advice - HOM:

HOM's workload is as follows:
- 80% lectures
- 16% Practicals
- 4% histology

### Actual Cambridge Medicine Advice - HOM - Lectures:

Convince yourself that it's super interesting and you'll do fine. There are a few super tricky concepts like capacitance, that might take you a while to get, but I don't really have good advice for HOM. Our Director of Studies is an amazing HOM supervisor, so I always went into our supervisions knowing nothing, and left fully understanding it. I have some very good HOM resources, but I didn't make any of them, so I don't think I have any right to share them publicly. I really have no advice here. I had an amazing HOM experience, all thanks to my supervisors for a lot of subjects, but I can't do justice to Hugh Matthews' teaching style and tips here. I'd just hope it's good in other colleges too!

Our ventilation lectures oversimplified what we needed to know, and voltage clamping is probably the hardest thing I looked at in HOM, which isn't that difficult. It gets progressively easier, because you just keep revisiting the same ideas. On the whole (apart from ventillation), our lecturers are amazing for HOM, and the clinical linker lectures just before exams are super amazing too even if they're not examinable.

### Actual Cambridge Medicine Advice - HOM - Practicals:
The practical guy... just listen to him... and you'll be fine.
Again we have some legendary college resources that I don't think I can put on the internet, but just read the handout once before your exam and you'll be fine. 

In the actual practical sessions, all you need to do to stay afloat is put your phone away when the guy at the front is speaking. Nothing else matters, you could literally accomplish 30% of the work and it doesn't matter. 

A lot of this seems quite difficult, I found this to be the hardest part of HOM, because sometimes you just learn a method for an experiment but have no idea why you're doing it all, which makes it harder to follow, but it's really not that bad if you pay attention to the guy at the front and the instructions.

Also don't let your partners in the lab peer pressure you into skipping the forewords and explanations, tell them to stop and let you read it. It's not the damn UCAT, you can read at whatever pace you want and take your time. 

It might potentially be worth doing the pre-reading for these practicals to get a better idea of what's going on, but it's not necessary to pass, it just makes the experience more enjoyable. I'd say that Dissection Room prep is the only pre-reading you HAVE to do, but skimming this and histology don't hurt.

### Actual Cambridge Medicine Advice - HOM - Histology:
I went to every histology session and didn't learn a single thing. For the mandatory pre-reading quizzes, I either committed all the prereading to my short term memory or chat GPT. I don't think pre-reading or doing any work in term time is particularly important, the stakes are very low until exam season. And when it comes to revising, I think that the content is super poorly explained in the handout and would highly recommend putting it into AI. 

That being said... even though I knew literally nothing (not exaggerating), until 2 days before my exam, it's very easy to do high yield cramming at a very chill pace. I wasn't stressed when I was revising for histology, I just put my head down for a few hours and did well, so don't worry about it too much. Definitely don't let the handout pre-reading consume you. 

Lots of people will cry about histology, and lots of people will say it's easy - it's not serious, and it's not hard, but it's also not *that* easy. It's about a day of work to learn the entire subject well, before exams. And it only counts for 3.333% of your overall grade so... make of that what you will.

*Note: My friends have also emphasised that you should make sure to do the pre-reading quiz to get 100% attendance every week before the deadline, and that the diagrams in the official uni mock (which they give you access to a few weeks before the exam) seem to repeat in the actual exam too. So that mock is probably very high yield. They also recommend doing someone else's anki the day before the exam to learn it, which is not the method I used, but I see where they're coming from. I personally incline more to the methodology of reading AI summaries of the handouts, as a revision technique for histology, especially if you're stressed for time.*

### Actual Cambridge Medicine Advice - MIMS:

The workload for MIMS is:
- 90% lectures 
- 9.5% practicals
- 0.5% PBL
  
### Actual Cambridge Medicine Advice - MIMS - Lectures:
Pay attention to the lectures for respiration, they're good. Everyone around you will tell you they are super hard, and whilst it's true that there's a disparity between the first like 3 or 4 lectures and the rest, it's actually not hard. People just think it is. In your second term there are some truely unintelligible lectures with some pretty mid/bad handouts, so I would advice using your supervisors and AI for help when you encounter these... word salads.

Don't fall for it when everyone else stresses obsessively, and just try and follow along in the lectures as best as you can. I actually think that one of the reasons why medics complain about medicine, is because it's easy to make conversation about it, but in doing so they seem to create their own stress.

Don't stress about memorising anything in term time, save all the names to memorise for the holidays, but make sure you understand everything conceptually in term time.

This is the only subject for which I think Anki is useful, but only for certain topics, like for example covering up parts of the respiration diagram and testing yourself on the stages, or having an Anki deck for all the molecules involved in the DNA lectures and another one for proteins.

These are the topics in MIMS:

Michaelmas:
- First lecture series is just nonsense
- Second lecture series is all just one respiration diagram, it's basically all about diabetes
- Third lecture series is just about cellular mechanisms like signalling and transport and stuff, and it's just a bunch of diagrams

Lent:
- First lecture series is about DNA
- Second lecture series is about Proteins
- Third lecture series is about the Cell Cycle.
- Fourth lecture series is about genetics

Easter:
- Didn't even revise nutrition, because I thought "the examiners have to set a standardised test for both the vets and medics and we learn different content, so it's unlikely to come up" - it didn't come up.

Essentially in MIMS you study "diabetes/respiration" in the first term and "cancer/genetics" in your second term, with a few extra side topics sprinkled between them, but those are the big 2.

Again I think I benefitted from having legendary supervisors, like the other 2 subjects, that took a really broad conceptual approach to everything, and so I just hope other colleges have the same level of teaching...

### Actual Cambridge Medicine Advice - MIMS - Practical:
This is easy.

Everyone will tell you it's hard, but they just didn't do any revision.

We had a good supervisor who went through everything that could come up, and when you learn the few experiments that just repeat, it's not difficult at all.

I can see how someone would think that it's difficult, but if you take a super conceptual teleological approach it isn't.

Don't pre-read for the actual practicals, but also make sure that you know **why** you are doing what you're doing and focus in the explanations that the demonstrators give beforehand.

### Actual Cambridge Medicine Advice - MIMS - PBL:
My presentation was full of jokes, that's my only tip, make it funny. Not because it's important to helping you pass (it's hard to fail and it doesn't make a difference), but because PBL is the most boring thing ever... you have to sit through 2 hours of boring student presentations. Please make your presentation interesting for the sanity of your peers.

Other than that, fake GMC required subject, easy to pass, the content is skippable and low yield, but interesting. Don't bother trying to learn from the actual presentations, the notes that you get afterwards are high yield enough.

### Actual Cambridge Medicine Advice - SECHI:
Now we get to the fake subjects. Just do the bare minimum for SECHI, and then write a decent essay for your 2 coursework subjects and you'll be fine.

### Actual Cambridge Medicine Advice - FEBP:
I hated the first lecture and didn't go to the rest (the only subject for which I skipped lectures), and then learnt it all 2 days before my exam, very easy, there's some good resources, particularly isoc ones I think, which succinctly sum it up. I don't think I can share them because I didn't make them though. Just do some practice and you'll be fine, it sounds scary, and it was particularly scary for us because it was on eid, but it's fine.

### Actual Cambridge Medicine Advice - PFP:
Just wear formal-ish clothes to your placement and in your coursework write the answers they want to hear, not what you really think, and you'll be fine.
